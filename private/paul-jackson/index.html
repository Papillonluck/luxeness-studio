Two sample LinkedIn posts and ideas for your next ones

From our conversation on 29 October.

Sample post one

At Claridge’s, guests took afternoon tea while we dug five storeys beneath them for a new spa and pool.

It went 22 metres down, under the 1920s Art Deco wing, and the hotel stayed fully open throughout.

This renovation, at Mandarin Oriental, Hong Kong, is a little like that project, in a much shorter time frame.

No basements being dug this time, but quite a lot of structural work nonetheless.

We closed in June and reopened four months later, with pretty much all the public areas renovated, plus a few new restaurants.

We have almost 300 rooms out of 419 at the moment. The rest come back through to May, followed by two new floors of wellness.

What guests see is the work of five design studios, from Malherbe Paris to André Fu.

What they feel is the work of a team who have carried this house through every phase.

Doing this a second time, I think you worry less about the day the work is finished, and more about every day in between.

A guest doesn’t need the whole house to be complete. They need every part that is open to feel as though it always was.

Read the notes on this post

Why it opens here. The Claridge’s dig is one of the best-known hotel projects of recent years, filmed by the BBC, and only you can tell it from the inside. It sets up the reopening here in your own words: a little like that project, in a much shorter time frame.

What comes from you. The comparison is yours, in your words: a little like the Claridge’s project, in a much shorter time frame; no basements being dug, but quite a lot of structural work nonetheless; closing in June and reopening four months later, with pretty much all the public areas renovated, plus a few new restaurants; almost 300 rooms out of 419, with the rest coming back through to May; and the two floors of wellness.

What I researched and added. The Claridge’s dig itself: five storeys and 22 metres down, beneath the 1920s Art Deco wing, for the spa and pool, while the hotel stayed fully open. The five design studios come from the reopening coverage.

Why the designers and the team are named. The post is about the house and the people who shaped it, so you are its guide rather than its subject. The comparison with Claridge’s is told in your own understated words.

Why it ends where it does. The last line turns what you have now done twice into something any leader reopening a hotel can take away: a guest doesn’t need the whole house to be complete, only every part that is open to feel as though it always was. That is the line people save and pass on.

What I would confirm with you. The afternoon tea image is mine. I had breakfast at Claridge’s myself while the dig was going on, so I know the hotel stayed open, but you will know the moment that best captures it. The closing thought, that a guest needs every part that is open to feel as though it always was, is my reading of what you have now done twice. A short voice note would tell me whether that is how you would put it.

Sample post two

Covid now looks like the easier time.

Someone said that to me recently, and I understand why.

But I have a different theory about when the world really changed.

I think the global financial crisis of 2009 was the catalyst. Before it, the economic ups and downs were cyclical.

Seventeen years on, we still live in an ongoing, volatile, unpredictable environment, and I don’t expect it to settle.

There is a saying, often called a Chinese curse: may you live in interesting times. That is certainly what is happening, globally.

Since 2009, all of us have been living in them.

That, I think, makes waiting for calm the riskiest plan a hotel can have.

Hong Kong is the proof.

The city went through a chequered time: the years before Covid, then a very long Covid, then two more years of recovery while the region was depressed.

Anyone waiting for certainty would still be waiting.

Mandarin Oriental, Hong Kong didn’t. No hotel reaches 63 years by waiting for calm.

A renovation of more than US$100 million began in September last year, while the city was still recovering.

On 1 October we reopened, and today Hong Kong is very much back.

We are taught to invest when times feel secure.

If the world won’t settle, I think the leaders in luxury hospitality who thrive will be the ones who learn to invest without that security.

Read the notes on this post

Why it opens here. Early in our call, I said that Covid almost looks like the easier time now. You had a different theory: that the world has changed since the global financial crisis, and that 2009 was the catalyst. It was the one moment you went against the common view, and a view against the grain is what makes a senior reader stop. So the post opens on the thought most people share, then turns to yours. A line the reader doesn’t expect makes them want to read on, and a view they can agree or disagree with invites them to reply. Those conversations are what carry a post beyond your own network.

What comes from you. The argument is yours, and wherever you gave the words, they are your words: the different theory; 2009 as the catalyst; the economic ups and downs being cyclical before it; living in an ongoing, volatile, unpredictable environment; may you live in interesting times, and that it is certainly what is happening, globally; Hong Kong’s chequered time, the very long Covid and the years of recovery while the region was depressed; the renovation starting in September last year; reopening on 1 October; and Hong Kong being very much back.

What I researched and added. Two facts, both checked: the renovation of more than US$100 million, from the announcement, and the hotel’s 63 years since it opened in 1963. The 63 years carry the argument, because a house that has stood that long is the natural proof that waiting for calm is not a plan. I also wrote "often called" before the Chinese curse, because the saying has no known Chinese source, and a well-read reader may know that.

Why the renovation sits in the middle. The reopening has already been announced. What only you can add is what it shows, so the facts are there as evidence for your argument rather than as news.

Why it ends where it does. The last line turns your view into something any leader in our industry can take away: if the world won’t settle, the ones who thrive will be those who learn to invest without that security. That is the line people save and pass on.

What I would confirm with you. A few lines are my reading of you rather than your words: the opening two lines, "Seventeen years on", "Since 2009, all of us have been living in them", "the riskiest plan a hotel can have", "Anyone waiting for certainty would still be waiting", "No hotel reaches 63 years by waiting for calm", and the closing thought. A short voice note on whether they sound like you is all I need.

How I wrote in your voice

How you talk

You are measured and exact. You correct yourself to stay accurate (“we did the first phase in four months. Not strictly true…”), so you would never let a post exaggerate. You reach for understatement rather than superlatives: “a little bit like the Claridge’s project”, “quite a lot of structural work nonetheless”, “a bit of a chequered time”.

You frame opinions politely rather than bluntly: “I think”, “I have a different theory”, “I think that’s true”. You hold a view firmly but never lecture, and you don’t tell people what to do.

You credit other people’s thinking (“what he articulated was…”) and find the positive in hard things: “I’m positive”, “a positive transition”, “still very vibrant… still thriving… still energetic”. You often speak in threes like that.

Under it all is a dry British wit: “my timing was good”, “that unfortunate Chinese curse, which is may you live in interesting times… But anyhow.”

In writing you are more formal still: “Dear Papillon”, “Kind regards”. Your own captions sound like a host: “Up with the lark at Claridge’s this morning as we unveil…”, “Delighted to welcome…”, “Be sure to take a close look…”. They’re warm and celebratory, with no exclamation marks and no hype.

What this means for the posts

Both posts are written in that voice. The first is told in your own understated wording (“no basements being dug, but quite a lot of structural work nonetheless”), credits the designers and the team rather than yourself, and offers its insight as “I think”.

The second turns to your theory in your own words, “I have a different theory”, and offers a view rather than a verdict: waiting for calm is, you think, the riskiest plan a hotel can have. Its closing thought is offered as your view, not an instruction to the reader, and your own wit, “may you live in interesting times”, carries the argument.

Each ending is still a strong, saveable opinion, but it’s your opinion, offered rather than imposed.

Why the posts look the way they do

LinkedIn is read on a phone, at speed, so each post is shaped to be easy to read and hard to scroll past.

Every choice is made for what happens after someone reads it. When a reader saves a post, shares it, comments on it or likes it, LinkedIn shows it to more people. Over time, that is how a following builds, among the owners, peers and partners whose attention matters to you.

The first line has one job: to make a busy reader stop. It opens on something unexpected, afternoon tea above a five-storey dig, or that Covid now looks like the easier time, without giving away where the post is going.

After that, each paragraph carries one idea, rarely more than two sentences, with space around it. An opening line, a short passage that explains it, then a line that lands it: that rhythm keeps the reader moving down the screen.

The last line is the thought worth keeping: something any leader in luxury hospitality can take away and use. It is what makes a reader save or share the post, and what gives them something to say in the comments.

How we would begin

You already do the hardest part on Instagram: you notice the moments that matter, from the taxi drivers at the Main Driveway to a celebrity fan’s atelier. LinkedIn adds the thinking behind them, for an entirely different audience: the owners, investors and peers who shape our industry. I write that for you.

It also takes less of your time than you might think. We begin with your Authority Foundation: one 45-minute conversation that picks up where we left off, to set your story, positioning and voice. Then come two reviews of no more than 20 minutes each, and within a week your LinkedIn profile would be optimised to establish you as an authority and grow your following, so the right people see straight away who you are and why your thinking matters.

From then on, it is around 20 minutes a week, and you will never face a blank page: the research, ideas, thinking and drafting are mine, and you simply confirm that each piece reflects your view.

We could begin in the first week of January, once the festive season at the hotel is behind you, so the reopening is told while it is still unfolding, from the rooms returning to the two floors of wellness.
