Two sample posts and a few subject ideas

From our conversation on 29 October.

Sample post one

Covid didn’t change the world. 2009 did.

Most people call the pandemic the turning point. I have a different theory: I think the financial crisis was the real break. Before it, our industry lived by cycles: you rode out the downturn, invested in the upturn, and could usually tell which one you were in. Since then we have lived in an ongoing, volatile, unpredictable environment, and I don’t expect it to settle.

There is an old Chinese curse: may you live in interesting times. Since 2009, all of us have.

That, I think, makes waiting for calm the riskiest plan a hotel can have.

Hong Kong is the proof. The city had a chequered run of years before Covid, then a very long Covid, then two more years of recovery while the region stayed depressed. Anyone waiting for certainty would still be waiting.

This hotel didn’t wait. A renovation of more than US$100 million began at our flagship in September last year, while the city was still finding its feet. On 1 October we reopened, and today Hong Kong is very much back.

I think the leaders who do well from here will be the ones who never stopped building.

Notes on this post

Why it opens here. On our call you said you had a different theory: that the world changed with the financial crisis, not with Covid. It was the one view you offered that runs against the common one, and that is what makes a senior reader stop.

Why the renovation sits in the middle. The reopening has already been announced. What only you can add is what it shows, so the facts are there as evidence for your argument rather than as news.

What I would confirm with you. The closing thought, and the line that waiting for calm is the riskiest plan a hotel can have, are my reading of your view. A short voice note would tell me whether you would put it that strongly.

Sample post two

We reopened Mandarin Oriental, Hong Kong on 1 October with more than a hundred rooms still to come.

I have led a project like this once before, at Claridge’s. This one is a little like it, in a much shorter time frame. No basements being dug this time, but quite a lot of structural work nonetheless. We closed in June and reopened four months later, with nearly every public space renovated and a few new restaurants.

Almost 300 of our 419 rooms are welcoming guests today. The rest return through to May, followed by two new floors of wellness.

What guests see is the work of Malherbe Paris in the public spaces, Steve Leung Design Group in the Mandarin Grill + Bar, and Jeffrey A. Wilkes, André Fu Studio and Thierry Lemaire in the rooms and suites. What they feel is the work of a team who have carried this house through every phase.

Doing this a second time, I think you worry less about the day the work is finished, and more about every day in between. A guest doesn’t need the whole house to be complete. They need every part that is open to feel as though it always was.

Notes on this post

Why it opens here. Reopening with more than a hundred rooms still to come is the detail a fellow hotelier will stop for, and the one only you can explain.

Why the designers and the team are named. The post is about the house and the people who shaped it, so you are its guide rather than its subject. The comparison with Claridge’s is told in your own understated words.

What I would confirm with you. The closing thought, that a guest needs every part that is open to feel as though it always was, is my reading of what you have now done twice. A short voice note would tell me whether that is how you would put it.

How I wrote in your voice

How you talk

You are measured and exact. You correct yourself to stay accurate (“we did the first phase in four months. Not strictly true…”), so you would never let a post exaggerate. You reach for understatement rather than superlatives: “a little bit like the Claridge’s project”, “quite a lot of structural work nonetheless”, “a bit of a chequered time”.

You frame opinions politely rather than bluntly: “I think”, “I have a different theory”, “I think that’s true”. You hold a view firmly but never lecture, and you don’t tell people what to do.

You credit other people’s thinking (“what he articulated was…”) and find the positive in hard things: “I’m positive”, “a positive transition”, “still very vibrant… still thriving… still energetic”. You often speak in threes like that.

Under it all is a dry British wit: “my timing was good”, “that unfortunate Chinese curse, which is may you live in interesting times… But anyhow.”

In writing you are more formal still: “Dear Papillon”, “Kind regards”. Your own captions sound like a host: “Up with the lark at Claridge’s this morning as we unveil…”, “Delighted to welcome…”, “Be sure to take a close look…”. They’re warm and celebratory, with no exclamation marks and no hype.

What this means for the posts

Both posts are written in that voice. The first opens with your theory in your own words, “I have a different theory”, and offers a view rather than a verdict: waiting for calm is, you think, the riskiest plan a hotel can have. Its closing thought is offered as your view, not an instruction to the reader, and your own wit, “may you live in interesting times”, carries the argument.

The second is told in your own understated wording (“no basements being dug this time, but quite a lot of structural work nonetheless”), credits the designers and the team rather than yourself, and offers its insight as “I think”.

Each ending is still a strong, saveable opinion, but it’s your opinion, offered rather than imposed.

A few subject ideas

Each idea ends on a question rather than an answer, because the answer is yours. Finding it is what our next conversation would be for.

  • 01

    The first welcome isn’t at the front door.

    Your team at the Main Driveway, morning and afternoon, thanking Hong Kong’s taxi drivers. Who does a guest’s first impression really belong to?

  • 02

    Five studios, one house.

    Malherbe Paris, Steve Leung, Jeffrey A. Wilkes, André Fu and Thierry Lemaire each shaped part of the hotel. How do you keep one flagship from feeling like five?

  • 03

    Wellness, the second time.

    Two new floors open next year, and you were GM when Claridge’s opened its spa. What has changed in what a luxury guest expects from wellness?

  • 04

    A reopening is a pre-opening with guests already inside.

    You led the opening of Mandarin Oriental, Sanya. What carries over, and what doesn’t?

  • 05

    The guests who come back.

    Vivienne Tam’s bespoke atelier at the hotel. What do the hotel’s most loyal fans notice first after a renovation?

You already do the hardest part on Instagram: you notice the people and moments that make the hotel what it is. LinkedIn simply adds the thinking behind them, and I write that for you.

From here, it is one 45-minute conversation that picks up where we left off, then two reviews of no more than 20 minutes each, and your profile would be ready within a week of that conversation. You will never face a blank page: the research, ideas, thinking and drafting are mine, and you simply confirm that each piece reflects your view.

The groundwork could begin in the first week of January, once the festive season at the hotel is behind you, and well ahead of the rooms returning and the two floors of wellness opening.
